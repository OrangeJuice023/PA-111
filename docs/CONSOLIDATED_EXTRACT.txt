How to use these notes for essay exams
Avoid treating the theories as isolated definitions. A stronger essay explains the problem a theory responds 
to, the part of organizational life it clarifies, and the limits that led managers or later theorists to look beyond 
it. For example, Taylor explains how measured standards can improve efficiency; Human Relations shows 
why social relationships and employee needs also affect performance; the Systems and Contingency 
approaches then explain why both people and processes must be understood within a larger setting.
Essay pattern:  Make a clear claim, explain the relevant theory, connect or contrast it with another 
theory, apply both to a concrete organizational situation, and end with a qualified conclusion about when 
each approach is useful.

1. History of Administration
Historical origins • The management discipline • Organization in management
1.1 Administration and management as long-standing practices
Management did not begin when it became an academic subject. Large collective undertakings required 
people to plan work, divide responsibilities, coordinate resources, direct workers, and check results long 
before modern management theories existed. The construction of the Egyptian pyramids, irrigation and 
waterway systems, roads, bridges, the Great Wall of China, and Roman public works all depended on 
organized labor and administration.
The important distinction is between management as a practice and management as a formal discipline. 
Societies had administrative practices for thousands of years; systematic theories developed later as 
organizations, trade, and production became more complex. Studying history therefore helps explain why 
present-day management emphasizes coordination, rules, specialization, records, authority, and 
performance.

These examples show that administration developed as a response to practical collective problems. Written 
rules, division of labor, incentives, and standardized processes each solved a different coordination 
problem; none alone explains every aspect of an organization.
1.3 Trade, specialization, and the emergence of the management discipline
Adam Smith’s account of division of labor explained how specialization can increase output. When workers 
concentrate on separate stages of production, they can become proficient in a narrower task, reduce the 
time spent switching between tasks, and lower training costs. The same arrangement, however, creates a 
greater need for coordination: specialized activities must be brought together for the organization to 
produce a complete result.
Charles Babbage later developed this logic by arguing that task complexity should be matched to the level 
of skill required. Highly trained and expensive workers should be assigned to tasks that genuinely require 
their expertise, while routine steps can be performed by workers with less specialized training. This can 
reduce costs and make work easier to standardize or mechanize. It also raises a recurring issue: efficiency 
gains must be weighed against the quality and meaning of work.
The spread of printing, expanding trade, new commercial wealth, accounting, and more complex 
enterprises helped create demand for professional managerial roles. Management became a more explicit 
subject of study as organizations needed repeatable methods for organizing work across many people and 
activities.
1.4 Public administration and the study of administration
Public administration has always existed as part of government, but Woodrow Wilson observed that 
administration received less intellectual attention than its practical importance warranted. Administration is 
the operational work through which public decisions are carried out. It deserves systematic study because 
government objectives depend on how authority, personnel, procedures, and resources are organized in 
practice.
The history of public administration also shows that administration is shaped by its environment. 
Waterways, taxation, military affairs, public infrastructure, and services create different technical and social 
demands. This historical variation prepares the ground for a central theme in modern management: an 
organizational arrangement should be judged not only by its internal design but also by the problem it must 
solve and the context in which it operates.
1.5 Organization and management
An organization is a structured arrangement of people, activities, responsibilities, and resources brought 
together to achieve objectives. Management is the process of directing and coordinating those resources 
and activities so the objectives can be achieved effectively and efficiently. The two concepts are connected 
but not identical: organization concerns how the parts are arranged, while management concerns how 
those parts are guided and coordinated over time.
Organizations may be public, private, or nonprofit. They differ in purpose, accountability, resources, and the 
people they serve, yet all must coordinate work, make decisions, motivate participants, respond to 
conditions, and assess results. Public organizations, in particular, cannot define performance only in terms 
of profit; they must also consider public purpose, fairness, legality, and service quality.
Connection to the next division:  Historical practice established recurring problems - efficiency, 
coordination, authority, cooperation, and adaptation. The evolution of management thought can be read 
as a series of different answers to those problems.

2.1 Classical School
The Classical School concentrates on rational organization: divide work, define responsibilities, establish 
authority, create procedures, and improve the use of time and resources. Its lasting contribution is the 
recognition that organizational performance is not automatic; it depends on deliberate design. Its limitation 
appears when efficiency and formal control are treated as sufficient explanations of human behavior.
Division of labor: Adam Smith and Charles Babbage
Smith argued that specialization can expand production because workers become more proficient at 
individual tasks. Babbage developed the argument by matching the skill required for a task with the level of 
labor needed to perform it. Together, these ideas explain lower training costs, less idle time, and the 
possibility of mechanization. However, fragmented work creates dependencies between specialized stages, 
so the organization needs coordination and must consider whether repetitive roles limit initiative or interest.
Henry Metcalfe: administration through records
At Frankford Arsenal, Henry Metcalfe encountered inadequate written production records. He promoted 
systematic records of events and work, including a card system that allowed information to be classified and 
consolidated. His view was that administration could be studied by recording what happens, identifying 
patterns, and learning from experience. The useful distinction is: Metcalfe emphasizes recording 
organizational activity; Taylor emphasizes measuring work performance against standards.
Frederick Winslow Taylor: Scientific Management
Taylor argued that work should not depend mainly on habit, guesswork, or a “rule of thumb.” Managers 
should study each task systematically, determine an effective method, select and train workers for the work, 
and connect performance to clear standards and incentives. Scientific Management was intended to require 
a “mental revolution” by both managers and workers: instead of fighting over how a fixed surplus is divided, 
they should cooperate to increase the total surplus through higher productivity.
Taylor identified soldiering - workers deliberately limiting their pace - as a problem. He argued that worker 
behavior could be affected by fear that higher output would threaten jobs, as well as by management

practices that failed to reward productive effort. His response was to study work and create reliable 
standards. In the Bethlehem Steel experiments, work was divided into elements and timed; in the shovel 
experiment, the appropriate load was identified as about 21 pounds. The lecture reports that the cost of 
shoveling per ton fell from roughly 7–8 cents to 3–4 cents, even after accounting for additional 
administrative and equipment costs.

5.Develop a science for each element of work instead of relying on informal rules of thumb.
6.Scientifically select, train, and develop workers so their abilities fit the work.
7.Bring the scientifically developed method and the properly selected worker together.
8.Divide responsibility: management plans and develops methods, while workers perform the work with

Time study supplies the evidence for a standard; the standard makes performance measurable; 
measurement can then be connected to rewards. Taylor’s differential piece-rate system gives a higher rate 
for meeting the required standard and a lower rate for falling below it. This is meant to encourage output 
and quality, although a system that relies too heavily on quotas or penalties can encourage gaming, conflict, 
or unsafe behavior.
Taylor also argued that scientific methods must be improved when evidence supports a better approach. A 
standard is not necessarily permanent: a proposed method should be tested, compared with the current 
method, and adopted if it works better. Scientific Management therefore contains a potential cycle of 
measurement and continuous improvement, not merely a demand to work faster.
Gantt: training, leadership, and the task-and-bonus system
Henry L. Gantt built on the classical concern with productivity but placed greater emphasis on teaching and 
leading workers. In the task-and-bonus system, an instructor helps develop the required knowledge and 
habits, a clear task gives the worker a standard, and a bonus rewards attainment. Compared with Taylor’s 
differential piece-rate system, Gantt’s approach retained a day wage and added a bonus for meeting the 
task, with a higher piece-rate possible when output exceeded the standard.
Gantt’s contribution provides a bridge within the classical tradition toward later attention to people: 
managers should not only set standards and measure work; they should teach, develop, and cooperate with 
workers. His discussion of business responsibility also invites a broader question than profit alone: whether 
the organization’s activity serves the community. A manager therefore needs to consider both technical 
performance and the human and social consequences of the way work is organized.
Church and Alford: Systematic Management
Alexander Hamilton Church and Leon P. Alford emphasized methodical organization of work and the 
integration of operations with cost accounting. By tracking how resources and expenses relate to production 
stages, management can understand not only whether output is increasing but also where costs arise. Their 
approach can be understood as analytical and systematic while retaining a need to accommodate people 
and operating conditions. It illustrates that careful measurement and financial control need not mean 
ignoring context or relationships.
Henri Fayol: Administrative Management
Fayol shifted attention from the individual task to general management across the organization. He 
identified six broad industrial activities: technical production; commercial buying and selling; financial 
acquisition and use of capital; security of people and property; accounting; and managerial activity. His core 
managerial functions are commonly summarized as planning, organizing, commanding or directing, 
coordinating, and controlling. The essential point is that managing a whole enterprise requires more than 
improving one worker’s task; activities and departments must be aligned.
Fayol’s fourteen principles provide a vocabulary for that alignment:
9.Division of work: Specialization can improve proficiency.
10. Authority and responsibility: The right to direct must be matched by accountability.
11. Discipline: Rules and agreements must be fairly observed.
12. Unity of command: An employee should receive direct orders from one supervisor.
13. Unity of direction: Activities with one objective should follow one coordinated plan.
14. Subordination of individual interests: Private interests should not undermine the organization’s 
legitimate common purpose.

Fayol’s principles are useful guidelines, not a substitute for judgment. Unity of command may prevent 
conflicting instructions, for example, but highly specialized or project-based work can require cross-
functional cooperation. Fayol himself recognized that the appropriate balance between centralization and 
decentralization depends on context, a point that links classical administration to the Contingency 
Perspective.
Max Weber: bureaucracy and legitimate authority
Weber’s ideal type of bureaucracy organizes authority through formal rules and offices rather than through 
personal favor. Its characteristic features include specialization, hierarchy, written records, clearly defined 
duties, technical qualifications, impersonal rules, a career structure, and a separation between the office 
and the private property of the officeholder. The official obeys the impersonal order attached to the office, 
not simply the personal wishes of whoever occupies it. Weber associated this structure with rational-legal 
authority.
Weber distinguished three bases of legitimate authority: rational-legal authority (belief in formal rules and 
offices), traditional authority (custom and established tradition), and charismatic authority (belief in a 
leader’s exceptional qualities). These are categories of legitimacy; they should not be confused with the 
structural characteristics of bureaucracy.
Bureaucracy can support continuity, predictability, specialization, documented decisions, and equal 
treatment under rules. In public administration, these features help constrain arbitrary personal power and 
make official conduct reviewable. However, the ideal type should not be mistaken for a guarantee of good 
administration. Formal authority may conflict with professional expertise (Parsons); the attitudes of 
employees toward rules matter (Bendix); responsibility, values, and consultation also matter (Corpuz and 
Friedrich); and cooperation may sometimes support productivity better than competition over individual 
performance records (Blau).
Other critiques expose dysfunctional consequences. Robert Merton explains how rules can become ends in 
themselves: employees may comply with procedure even when doing so undermines the original goal. 
Philip Selznick shows that subunits can pursue their own goals at the expense of the organization as a 
whole. Herbert Simon argues against the idea of one perfect administrative structure, emphasizing that 
different situations require different arrangements. These criticisms do not make rules useless; they show 
that rules need to remain connected to purpose, expertise, responsibility, and context.
Classical School: overall contribution and limitation
The Classical School supplied organizations with methods for dividing work, recording activity, setting 
standards, clarifying authority, coordinating departments, and maintaining continuity. Its limitation is the 
temptation to treat workers mainly as instruments of production or to assume that a formally rational 
arrangement will automatically produce effective outcomes. The Human Relations School grew partly from 
the recognition that informal groups, social expectations, satisfaction, and employee development affect the 
way formal systems actually work.
Classical-school essay link:  Taylor asks how work can be measured and standardized; Fayol asks 
how management can coordinate the enterprise; Weber asks how authority can be made impersonal 
and predictable. Their approaches complement one another, but their limits become visible when 
workers’ social needs, professional judgment, and changing circumstances are ignored.

2.2 Human Relations School and motivation
The Human Relations School shifts the explanation of organizational performance toward people’s 
attitudes, relationships, informal groups, morale, and motivation. It does not mean that structure, standards, 
or pay no longer matter. Rather, it argues that technical conditions and economic incentives cannot fully 
explain what employees do. Organizations are social systems as well as systems for producing goods or 
services.
The Hawthorne Investigation
The Hawthorne studies at Western Electric’s Hawthorne Works in Chicago ran from 1927 to 1932 and 
involved researchers including Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger. The illumination experiments explored 
whether changes in light affected output; the relay assembly test room considered working hours, rest 
periods, conditions, and group relations; the interviewing program explored employee attitudes; and the 
bank wiring test room examined behavior under relatively normal working conditions. Productivity changes 
could not be explained by physical conditions alone.
The studies contributed to the Human Relations Movement by drawing attention to informal groups, group 
norms, employee attitudes, participation, two-way communication, cooperation, and morale. The often-used 
term “Hawthorne effect” refers to the possibility that people change their behavior when they know they are 
being observed or given special attention. For essay purposes, the broader lesson is safer and more useful 
than claiming that attention alone explains productivity: managers must study the social context of work as 
well as the task and physical setting.
Maslow: hierarchy of needs
Maslow proposed five broad levels of need: physiological needs (food, rest, and basic survival), safety 
(security and stability), love and belonging (relationships and acceptance), esteem (respect, competence, 
achievement, and recognition), and self-actualization (developing one’s potential). The model suggests that 
unmet needs influence behavior and that people may shift attention as needs become sufficiently satisfied. 
It is a useful framework for understanding why wages alone may not motivate every employee. It should not 
be treated as a rigid sequence that applies identically to every person or situation.
Herzberg: Two-Factor Theory
Herzberg distinguishes hygiene factors from motivators. Hygiene factors - such as salary, working 
conditions, security, policy, supervision, and workplace relations - help prevent dissatisfaction when 
adequate, but their presence does not necessarily create strong motivation. Motivators - such as 
achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth, and meaningful work - are more directly connected to 
satisfaction and the desire to perform. The managerial implication is to address poor conditions while also 
designing work that offers achievement and development.
McGregor: Theory X and Theory Y
Theory X assumes that employees tend to dislike work, avoid responsibility, and require close control or 
external pressure. Theory Y assumes that work can be natural and satisfying, and that employees may 
seek responsibility and use creativity when conditions support them. These are not fixed types of people; 
they are contrasting managerial assumptions. A Theory X manager tends toward close direction and 
control, while a Theory Y manager invests in participation, autonomy, and opportunities to develop. 
Management assumptions can become self-reinforcing: a highly controlling environment may discourage 
initiative, while a supportive one may help employees demonstrate responsibility.
Argyris: personality and organization
Chris Argyris examines how people may develop from relatively passive, dependent, and externally 
controlled behavior toward greater activity, independence, confidence, self-control, flexibility, and 
responsibility. Rigid structures, narrow repetitive jobs, limited delegation, and close supervision may restrict 
this development. Argyris therefore recommends broadening responsibilities, increasing task variety, 
allowing greater participation, and improving human relationships. When mature employees face restrictive 
conditions, they may escape (for example, by resigning or being absent), fight (through individual or 
collective resistance), or adapt by becoming apathetic and disengaged.
Argyris connects Human Relations to organizational design: motivation is not only an individual trait; it is 
also affected by how work and authority are arranged. This creates a useful contrast with narrow

Goal-Setting Theory
Goal-Setting Theory holds that clear, specific, challenging, and measurable goals can direct effort and 
improve performance, especially when accompanied by deadlines, feedback, and commitment. Goals 
clarify what is expected; feedback helps employees understand progress and adjust their behavior. 
Commitment is stronger when goals are communicated, accepted, consistent with organizational purpose, 
and supported by confidence that the task can be performed. The slides refer to “self-efficiency”; the 
commonly used term for confidence in one’s ability to perform a task is self-efficacy.
Goal setting can fail when organizational and managerial goals conflict, when difficult goals encourage 
excessive risk-taking, or when employees lack the skills and resources needed to achieve them. The 
lecture also cautions against assuming that goal-setting by itself necessarily improves job satisfaction. 
Challenging goals should therefore be matched to capability, support, and the organization’s broader 
objectives.
Equity Theory
Equity Theory explains motivation through perceived fairness. People compare the outcomes they receive - 
such as compensation, recognition, advancement, or self-worth - with their inputs, including effort, 
commitment, enthusiasm, experience, loyalty, personal sacrifice, and flexibility. They may compare their 
current situation with their own past experience inside or outside the organization, or with another person’s 
experience inside or outside the organization.
The comparison is relational rather than purely absolute. If a person believes that their outcome-to-input 
ratio is lower than a reference person’s, they may feel under-rewarded; if the ratios appear equal, they 
perceive equity; if their ratio appears higher, they may feel over-rewarded or experience tension. Both 
perceived under-reward and over-reward can affect motivation. The managerial lesson is that reward 
systems need to be fair and explainable, and managers must consider employees’ perceptions of workload, 
recognition, and treatment rather than assuming that a reward has the same meaning for everyone.
Alderfer: ERG Theory
Alderfer groups needs into Existence, Relatedness, and Growth. Existence includes basic material and 
physical safety needs; Relatedness includes significant interpersonal relationships and recognition; Growth 
concerns personal development and the realization of potential. ERG Theory condenses Maslow’s five 
categories into three and allows more than one need to be active at the same time.
Its frustration-regression principle adds an important nuance: when a higher-level need is blocked, a person 
may place greater emphasis on a lower-level need that seems more attainable. For example, an employee 
who cannot obtain growth opportunities may become more concerned with pay, security, or workplace 
relationships. ERG therefore offers a more flexible picture than a strictly step-by-step hierarchy.

2.3 Modern School: modern management theory and the Systems Approach
Modern management theory treats organizations as complex arrangements whose performance depends 
on evidence, employee behavior, processes, technology, and their environment. Compared with a narrow 
focus on financial gain, modern management recognizes that employees may also work for satisfaction, 
development, meaningful activity, and desired ways of life. Managers can use mathematical and statistical 
techniques to diagnose problems, compare alternatives, and support decisions with data.
The slides identify several benefits: modern approaches can boost productivity by analyzing performance; 
aid decision-making by identifying problems and evaluating solutions; improve employee engagement by 
considering varying needs; promote objectivity by using evidence rather than personal opinion alone; and 
enable adaptability by recognizing internal and external influences. These benefits depend on how evidence 
is interpreted and used; quantitative measures are tools for judgment, not replacements for it.
Systems Approach
The Systems Approach views an organization as a set of interconnected and interdependent parts working 
toward common objectives. Ludwig von Bertalanffy introduced the systems idea in General Systems Theory 
through the study of relationships between organisms and their environments; the approach was later 
applied to business and administrative organizations. Rather than diagnosing one department in isolation, 
managers ask how its actions affect the whole organization.
Organizations are open systems because they exchange resources and information with their external 
environment. Their basic model is: Inputs → Transformation Process → Outputs → Feedback. Inputs 
include employees, money, materials, technology, information, and other resources. The transformation 
process includes management, production, communication, and decision-making activities. Outputs include 
products, services, performance, customer satisfaction, and other results. Feedback is information about 
performance that helps managers correct problems and improve the system.
Departments are subsystems. In a manufacturing organization, purchasing obtains materials, production 
transforms them, and sales connects finished output to customers. A supply failure can delay production 
and then affect sales; a sales forecast can also change purchasing and production plans. These 
dependencies illustrate interdependence. A holistic perspective therefore considers the whole; attention to 
the external environment recognizes customers, competitors, regulation, technology, and economic 
conditions; feedback and adaptation help the organization adjust; and common objectives give the parts a 
shared direction.
The Systems Approach is different from simply drawing an organization chart. Its main claim is that the 
relationships among components are as important as the components themselves. It helps explain why 
optimizing one department can harm overall performance if the department’s decisions create problems 
elsewhere.

Modern management and Systems Approach: relationship
Modern management is the broader orientation toward evidence, people, and organizational complexity. 
The Systems Approach is one perspective within that orientation: it directs attention to interdependence, 
open-system relationships, and feedback. The two reinforce each other because evidence about outputs 
and the environment can be used to diagnose where the system is failing and where changes are needed.
2.4 Emerging Management Perspectives
Contingency Perspective
The Contingency Perspective builds on the Systems Approach but adds that management decisions must 
fit the circumstances. It rejects the idea that one method works equally well in every organization or 
situation. Associated in the lecture with Fred Fiedler, the perspective asks managers to analyze the 
situation before selecting a structure, leadership style, control system, or operating practice.
Relevant contingencies include the nature of the task, the size of the organization, employee skills and 
experience, organizational structure, technology, external environment, leadership situation, goals, and 
available resources. A highly competitive or rapidly changing industry may require flexibility, while a stable 
setting may allow more routine procedures and structured operations. The point is not that flexible 
management is always best; it is that the approach should fit the task and environment.
Its advantages include flexibility, closer attention to differences among organizations, stronger responses to 
environmental change, and more informed decision-making. Its limitations are practical: it can be difficult to 
identify every relevant factor, managers may disagree on which approach fits best, analyzing alternatives 
takes time and resources, and the perspective provides no simple formula for solving every problem.
Chaos Theory
Chaos Theory highlights complexity, sensitivity to initial conditions, and the limits of prediction. Henri 
Poincaré’s work is associated with the insight that small differences in starting conditions can lead to large 
differences in later outcomes. Meteorologist Edward Lorenz’s “Butterfly Effect” illustrates the same idea: a 
small change in one part of a complex system may contribute to substantial effects elsewhere. The point is 
not that every small event causes a crisis, but that interconnected systems can amplify some changes in 
ways that are difficult to forecast.
Organizations are complex because people, departments, technologies, markets, customers, competitors, 
and processes interact. They experience continuous change; small errors or decisions can have large 
effects; and managers cannot always predict outcomes precisely. At the same time, employees and teams 
may self-organize, develop new working patterns, and innovate without detailed instructions for every 
action.
For managers, Chaos Theory supports flexibility rather than dependence on rigid plans alone. Managers 
should encourage experimentation, respond quickly to emerging changes, accept uncertainty as part of 
organizational life, empower employees when appropriate, monitor emerging trends, and develop resilience 
so the organization can recover from unexpected events. This does not eliminate planning; it means plans 
need room for learning and revision.

3. Metaphors of Organization
Different lenses reveal different features of the same organization
A metaphor is a lens for interpreting an organization, not a literal claim that it is a machine, organism, brain, 
or political system. Each metaphor draws attention to some features and may hide others. Strong analysis 
compares lenses rather than assuming that one image fully explains the organization.
3.1 Organization as a machine
The machine metaphor emphasizes clearly defined jobs, standard procedures, hierarchy, rules, efficiency, 
and predictability. It connects closely to Scientific Management, Fayol’s administrative principles, and the 
formal structure of bureaucracy. It is useful when work is repeatable and consistency, safety, or reliable 
processing matters. Its danger is rigidity: if workers are treated only as parts of a machine, the organization 
may neglect initiative, social relationships, complex judgment, and changing conditions.
3.2 Organization as an organism
The organism metaphor emphasizes survival, adaptation, needs, and the relationship between an 
organization and its environment. It aligns with the Systems Approach because an open organization 
receives resources, responds to environmental conditions, and must maintain relationships with the people 
and institutions around it. It also connects to Contingency thinking: the structure and practices that fit one 
environment may not fit another. The metaphor reminds managers to ask whether the organization’s current 
arrangements match its environment and the needs of those who participate in it.
3.3 Organization as a brain
The brain metaphor highlights information, knowledge, learning, intelligence, and decision-making. It 
connects to Metcalfe’s systematic records, Taylor’s measurement, modern evidence-based management, 
and the feedback component of systems thinking. An organization learns when it uses performance 
information to identify errors, tests alternative methods, and updates its practices. Chaos Theory adds that 
this learning must continue because the setting may shift and outcomes may not be fully predictable.
3.4 Organization as a political system
The political-system metaphor focuses on power, competing interests, conflict, negotiation, and influence. It 
clarifies why formal goals do not always translate directly into action: managers, employees, departments, 
professional groups, unions, and outside stakeholders may have different interests. Taylor’s account of 
conflict between employers and workers, Weber’s analysis of authority, and Selznick’s concern that 
subunits may pursue their own goals all illustrate political dynamics. The metaphor is useful when analyzing 
who has influence, whose interests shape decisions, and how agreement is achieved or blocked.
3.5 Combining metaphors in analysis
Consider a public agency introducing a new permit process. The machine lens asks whether responsibilities 
and steps are clear and consistent. The organism lens asks whether the process fits citizens’ needs and the 
agency’s environment. The brain lens asks whether the agency uses processing-time data, complaints, and 
staff knowledge to improve the procedure. The political lens asks whose interests shape the new rules, who 
may resist them, and how disagreements are managed. Each lens reveals a different part of the same 
situation.
This is also a way to weave theories in an essay. Instead of listing four metaphors, explain why each one 
adds something the others miss. A machine metaphor can explain reliability but not all informal behavior; an 
organism metaphor shows adaptation but may understate internal conflict; a brain metaphor highlights 
learning but not necessarily power; a political metaphor explains conflict but may understate shared 
purpose. The most useful interpretation depends on the question being asked.

4. Functions of Administration
Planning • Implementation • Monitoring • Evaluation
The syllabus identifies four connected functions of administration. They are best understood as an ongoing 
cycle rather than isolated tasks. Planning establishes the intended result; implementation puts the plan into 
practice; monitoring tracks whether activities are proceeding as intended; evaluation assesses the 
outcomes and informs the next round of planning.
4.1 Planning
Planning defines the problem, intended objectives, actions, resources, responsibilities, and timeline. Goals 
need to be clear and measurable, but they must also be realistic, sufficiently challenging, and consistent 
with the organization’s wider purpose. Fayol’s administrative approach highlights planning as a managerial 
responsibility; Goal-Setting Theory explains why specific goals and feedback can direct effort; and the 
Contingency Perspective requires that a plan fit the task, the people, and the environment. A plan should 
identify assumptions and risks rather than treating the future as certain.
4.2 Implementation
Implementation turns decisions into action. It requires assigning responsibilities, coordinating departments, 
providing staff and other resources, communicating expectations, and developing the skills needed to 
perform the work. Taylor emphasizes scientific methods, training, and standards; Fayol stresses 
organization and coordination; Human Relations theories show why cooperation, employee needs, and 
participation also matter. The Contingency Perspective adds that the same implementation method may not 
fit every situation.
4.3 Monitoring
Monitoring tracks activities and outputs while implementation is underway. Managers compare actual 
progress with the plan, standards, and deadlines, and look for early signs of delay, poor quality, unfair 
treatment, or unintended consequences. Taylor’s measurement and Metcalfe’s records show the value of 
reliable information; the Systems Approach explains why feedback must travel between parts of the 
organization. Monitoring is not simply policing workers: it should help the organization find out what is 
happening and what needs adjustment.
4.4 Evaluation
Evaluation asks whether the action achieved its objectives and whether the results were effective, efficient, 
fair, and sustainable. It should consider not only immediate outputs but also the quality and wider 
consequences of the work. Equity Theory reminds evaluators to consider perceived fairness; Human 
Relations theories direct attention to employee and user experiences; Systems thinking asks how outcomes 
relate to inputs and processes; and Chaos Theory cautions that some effects may emerge later or through 
unexpected interactions. Evaluation should produce learning, not just a judgment of success or failure.
4.5 The functions as a feedback cycle
23. Plan: Define a public-service problem, the desired result, the resources required, and clear 
indicators of progress.
24. Implement: Assign responsibilities, communicate the process, coordinate units, and provide staff 
with the training and resources needed.
25. Monitor: Compare actual progress with the plan and collect information from records, staff, and 
service users.
26. Evaluate: Assess results and unintended effects; determine what should be continued, changed, or 
stopped.

ESSAY INTEGRATION
Connecting the course in an essay
The strongest answers do more than define a school of thought. They explain how an idea responds to an 
earlier limitation, what it reveals about organizations, and why it still needs to be applied with judgment.
Four useful threads
28. Efficiency and the human side of work: Smith and Babbage explain specialization; Taylor adds 
measurement, standards, training, and incentives; Gantt stresses teaching and cooperation; 
Hawthorne, Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor, and Argyris show why relationships, needs, assumptions, 
and development affect performance.
29. Authority and organizational responsibility: Fayol clarifies managerial coordination; Weber explains 
impersonal authority and bureaucracy; the critics show that rules can become dysfunctional, expertise 
can conflict with hierarchy, and responsibility requires values and judgment as well as formal 
procedure.
30. Motivation and fairness: Goal-Setting Theory links effort to clear goals and feedback; Equity Theory 
explains why perceived fairness shapes motivation; ERG, Maslow, and Herzberg explain why different 
needs and work conditions matter. These can be combined to explain why an incentive succeeds in 
one case but fails in another.
31. Whole system and changing context: The Systems Approach maps interdependence and feedback; 
the Contingency Perspective asks what fits the situation; Chaos Theory recognizes that complex 
systems can change unexpectedly. Together, they argue for evidence, context, flexibility, and learning 
rather than one universal formula.
A compact structure for a pure essay
Start with a defensible argument that answers the question directly. Explain the central theory in your own 
words, not just as a list of terms. Connect it to a related theory or an earlier school by naming what the 
second theory adds or challenges. Apply the connection to a concrete organization or administrative 
problem. Finally, qualify the argument by identifying a limitation or condition under which the approach may 
not work. This gives the essay a line of reasoning rather than a collection of definitions.
Final synthesis
The course’s overall movement is from the organization of work and authority toward a more complete 
understanding of people, systems, and uncertainty. Classical management remains useful for structure, 
efficiency, standards, and accountability. Human Relations shows that employee behavior depends on 
social relationships, needs, perceived fairness, and opportunities to develop. Modern management shows 
that decisions benefit from evidence and from understanding how organizational parts interact. Emerging 
perspectives remind managers to adapt methods to circumstances and to remain responsive when 
conditions are unpredictable. Effective administration combines these insights through planning, 
implementation, monitoring, and evaluation.
